Data & Forecast Design

A reproducible specification for defining the forecasting target, information available at forecast origin, candidate horizons, baselines, uncertainty, validation, and operational decision criteria.

Persistence is the mandatory benchmark. Demonstrate skill before escalating complexity.

Defines the forecasting problem and evaluation framework without prematurely locking the final target, model, architecture, or operational alert implementation.

01

Document Authority

This document defines the forecasting problem, target construction, forecast horizons, information availability, baselines, candidate model classes, validation methodology, uncertainty evaluation, and decision criteria for the Agri Telemetry & Forecasting Platform.

It translates the scientific representation defined in 00_project/PROJECT_CHARTER.md, 01_research/RESEARCH_FEASIBILITY.md, 02_product/PRD.md, and 03_scientific/DATA_SCIENTIFIC_SPEC.md into a reproducible forecasting specification.

It does not permanently select a specific forecasting model, machine-learning library, production serving architecture, message broker, database, or MLOps platform. Those choices remain subject to empirical evidence and, where architectural, ADRs.

02

Governing Forecasting Principle

Persistence must earn its defeat.

The system must first establish how difficult the forecasting problem actually is. A more complex model is justified only when it produces a meaningful and reproducible improvement over appropriate simpler baselines under a leakage-safe evaluation.

The objective is not to build the most sophisticated forecasting model. It is to determine whether additional modelling complexity produces useful predictive and operational value beyond what the temporal structure already provides.

03

Forecasting Objective

Estimate the future trajectory of the selected soil-water state from information available at a defined forecast origin.

$$\hat{Y}_{t+h|t}$$

Where Y is the selected soil-water state, t is forecast origin, h is forecast horizon, and Ŷ is the forecast made at t for t+h.

Where probabilistic forecasting is used, the system should estimate a conditional distribution rather than only a point estimate:

$$P(Y_{t+h} \mid X_{\le t})$$

The final target remains dependent on the Scientific Specification and empirical target-selection experiments.

05

Forecast Origin

Every forecast must have an explicit origin time. At origin t, the model may only use information available at or before t.

This may include:

  • historical target values;
  • previously observed weather;
  • previously derived variables;
  • known static or contextual information;
  • genuinely available weather forecasts;
  • scientifically legitimate known-future information.

Future realised observations must not enter an operational forecast.

06

08

Forecast Horizons

Candidate horizons include:

  • native observation interval;
  • 1 hour;
  • 6 hours;
  • 12 hours;
  • 24 hours;
  • 72 hours;
  • 168 hours / 7 days.

These are candidates rather than validated requirements. The final set depends on source cadence, target dynamics, persistence skill, forecast degradation, operational decision window, and future exogenous information availability.

Evaluate each horizon separately.

Researching
09

Forecast Types

9.1 Point Forecast

A single expected or central estimate used for error benchmarking, trajectory visualisation, and baseline comparison.

9.2 Probabilistic Forecast

Represents uncertainty using prediction intervals, quantile forecasts, or predictive distributions. The final method remains open.

9.3 Trajectory Forecast

A sequence of predictions across multiple future horizons. This is important because the operational objective concerns trajectory and potential threshold crossing rather than one isolated future value.

10

Required Baselines

10.1 Persistence

$$\hat{Y}_{t+h|t} = Y_t$$

Persistence is mandatory. For highly autocorrelated environmental signals, it can be difficult to beat at short horizons.

Decided

10.2 Seasonal / Climatological Baseline

Where sufficient history exists, a historical mean, median, quantile, or comparable-period baseline may be evaluated.

10.3 Rolling / EWMA Baseline

Rolling or exponentially weighted recent-history baselines may be evaluated where relevant.

10.4 Statistical Baselines

Candidate statistical models include ETS, ARIMA, SARIMA, and SARIMAX / dynamic regression.

11

Candidate Machine-Learning Models

Potential candidates include:

  • linear or regularised regression;
  • quantile regression;
  • random forest;
  • gradient-boosted trees;
  • quantile random forest;
  • other tree-based probabilistic approaches.

Model selection remains empirical. Lower complexity is preferred where it provides equivalent scientific and operational value.

Model Selection Open
12

Deep Learning

Potential sequence models include LSTM, GRU, temporal convolutional models, and transformer-based temporal models.

Deferred.

Introduce deep learning only if simpler models leave material, reproducible predictive structure unexplained and the additional complexity produces meaningful value.

Deferred

Deep learning must not be included merely to demonstrate AI/ML breadth.

13

Feature Design

Target History

Lagged target, rolling statistics, rate of change, and recent drying/wetting trend.

Precipitation

Recent rainfall, cumulative rainfall, time since rainfall, and rainfall intensity where supported.

Atmospheric State

Temperature, relative humidity, VPD, ET0, wind, and radiation.

Temporal Features

Hour, day of year, season, and cyclic representations where justified.

Contextual Variables

Soil properties, sensor depth, and crop/growth-stage information where available.

Features must earn inclusion through scientific relevance, availability at forecast origin, data quality, and out-of-sample value.

15

Leakage Prevention

Temporal leakage is a first-order scientific risk.

  • Random train/test splitting.
  • Future rolling statistics.
  • Future interpolation or imputation.
  • Scaling fitted on the full dataset.
  • Future-derived features.
  • Threshold selection using the final test.
  • Hyperparameter tuning against the final test.
  • Realised future weather in an operational experiment.
Rule. Every transformation used for forecasting must respect the temporal information boundary at forecast origin.

Walk-Forward Evaluation

Use rolling-origin or walk-forward procedures where practical. Repeatedly forecast future observations from historically available information. 

Train ────────► Forecast

Train ───────────────► Forecast
Train ─────────────────────► Forecast
Train ─────────────────────────────► Forecast

This better reflects deployment than random temporal splits.

18

Event-Aware Validation

Environmental events can create leakage or overly optimistic evaluation if split incorrectly.

Examples include:

  • rainfall events;
  • prolonged dry periods;
  • irrigation events;
  • sensor outages;
  • abrupt weather transitions.

Where practical, major contiguous events should not be artificially divided between training and test periods in a way that allows the same event structure to appear on both sides.

19

Multi-Site Validation

Temporal Generalisation

Same Site / Different Period

Train and test on different periods at the same station.

Spatial Generalisation

Unseen Site

Train using some sites and evaluate on an unseen site.

Good temporal performance at one station does not establish spatial generalisation to another site, soil, or climate.

20

Point-Forecast Metrics

Mean Absolute Error

$$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$$

Root Mean Squared Error

$$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$$

Mean Bias Error

$$\text{MBE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)$$

Skill Relative to Persistence

$$\text{Skill} = 1 - \frac{\text{Error}_{\text{model}}}{\text{Error}_{\text{persistence}}}$$

The exact skill metric and its interpretation must be defined consistently once experiments begin.

21

Metrics That Require Caution

R²

R² may be reported descriptively but is not sufficient evidence of forecasting usefulness. It can coexist with poor calibration, systematic bias, poor threshold detection, or poor long-horizon performance.

R² is therefore not a primary acceptance criterion.

22

Probabilistic Forecast Evaluation

If the system produces prediction intervals or quantiles, they must be evaluated directly.

Candidate metrics include:

  • empirical coverage;
  • interval width;
  • pinball loss;
  • CRPS;
  • weighted interval score (WIS).

22.1 Coverage

For a nominal (1−α) interval, compare empirical coverage with the stated nominal level.

22.2 Sharpness

Narrow intervals are useful only when appropriately calibrated. The goal is useful sharpness while maintaining acceptable empirical coverage.

23

Threshold / Event Metrics

Average forecast error is not sufficient when the product objective concerns threshold risk.

Where a scientifically defensible threshold exists, evaluate:

  • threshold-crossing detection;
  • false alerts;
  • missed events;
  • precision and recall where appropriate;
  • F1 where useful;
  • event lead time;
  • time-to-threshold error;
  • calibration of threshold-crossing probability.
24

Lead Time

Lead time is a key operational measure.

$$\text{LeadTime} = t_{\text{threshold}} - t_{\text{alert}}$$

A forecast with high classification accuracy may still have limited operational value if it arrives too late. Very early warnings may impose excessive false-alert burden.

25

False-Alert Burden

Do not optimise simply for maximum alert sensitivity.

  • Number of alerts.
  • False-alert rate.
  • Alert duration.
  • Repeated alerts for one event.
  • Lead time.
  • Missed events.

The acceptable burden remains a product decision. Open

26

Forecast Residuals

Residuals should be examined for:

  • bias;
  • autocorrelation;
  • changing variance;
  • seasonal structure;
  • systematic underprediction or overprediction;
  • behaviour during rainfall and dry-down;
  • behaviour near thresholds.

Residual autocorrelation matters because sequential forecast errors are not necessarily independent.

27

Prediction Intervals and Sequential Alerts

A sequence of observations outside a prediction interval must not automatically be interpreted using:

$$P(\text{N consecutive breaches}) = \alpha^N$$

Forecast residuals may be autocorrelated, so the independence assumption may not hold.

N-of-M rules are engineering persistence filters.

They are not automatically valid statistical false-alarm probabilities. Any persistence rule must be evaluated empirically.

Forecasting Ladder

LEVEL 0  Persistence

LEVEL 1  Seasonal / climatological baseline

LEVEL 2  Rolling / EWMA baseline

LEVEL 3  ETS / ARIMA / SARIMA

LEVEL 4  Dynamic regression / SARIMAX

LEVEL 5  Quantile regression / tree-based probabilistic models

LEVEL 6  More complex sequence models
only if justified

Stop climbing the ladder when additional complexity fails to produce material value.

29

Model Escalation Criteria

Promote complexity only with consistent improvement in meaningful dimensions:

  • lower point error;
  • better persistence skill;
  • threshold-event detection;
  • useful lead time;
  • uncertainty calibration;
  • narrower intervals at comparable coverage;
  • seasonal/site stability;
  • improved residual structure.

A small gain in one average metric is not automatically sufficient.

30

Model Rejection Criteria

Reject or defer when:

  • the model does not consistently beat persistence;
  • gains disappear under leakage-safe evaluation;
  • gains occur only at one horizon;
  • average error improves while threshold detection worsens;
  • uncertainty is poorly calibrated;
  • performance is unstable over time;
  • unavailable operational features are required;
  • complexity increases without decision value;
  • the dataset is insufficient for reliable selection.
Requires Evidence

Reproducibility Requirements

Every forecasting experiment must record dataset/source and version, retrieval reference/date, time period, target definition, sensor depth, unit, preprocessing, missing-data/QC policy, feature definitions, horizons, scenario, train/validation/test periods, walk-forward method, model, hyperparameters, random seed where applicable, code version, metrics, uncertainty/event metrics, assumptions, and limitations.

Evidence rule.

A forecast result without its experimental metadata is not considered reproducible evidence.

35

Final Test Protection

The final test period must not influence feature selection, model selection, hyperparameter tuning, threshold selection, horizon selection, or alert-rule selection.

The final test exists to provide an independent estimate of performance after development decisions are complete.

Minimum Evidence for Forecast Validation

Validation is not established merely because a model runs, a graph looks plausible, training loss decreases, tests pass, or a complex model produces predictions.

Required evidence:

  1. Scientifically defined target.
  2. Explicit forecast origin.
  3. Leakage control.
  4. Persistence evaluation.
  5. Chronological evaluation.
  6. Protected final test.
  7. Horizon-specific reporting.
  8. Uncertainty evaluation when probabilistic forecasts are claimed.
  9. Threshold/event evaluation where applicable.
  10. Reproducible results.
Requires Project Evidence
38

Scientific and Operational Interpretation

Forecast performance and operational usefulness are distinct. A model may lower MAE without improving threshold detection, improve average error but calibrate poorly, perform well short-term but lack useful longer-horizon lead time, or require unavailable future inputs.

Forecast accuracy is necessary evidence, but not by itself evidence of operational value.
